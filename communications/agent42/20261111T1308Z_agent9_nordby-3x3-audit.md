Key details:
- pp. 164–168 define the binary-rune system as ætt/group plus position. The standard Younger Futhark grouping is fuþork / hnias / tbmly, with epigraphic group numbering 321 and forward positions. Thus 3/3 = the third rune of fuþork = þ.
- pp. 185–187 use the Schleswig (DR Schl12) key as the clearest epigraphic demonstration and state that no alternative division/numbering has produced a secure interpretation in the Viking-Age or medieval epigraphic corpus surveyed.
- p. 180, fig. 59 lists N114 Torpo 3/3 -> þ and B672 Bryggen 3/3 3/3 -> þþ. Crucially, both are in Nordby's uncertain/uninterpreted group, not the convincing-inscription table.
- p. 141, fig. 45 describes N114's second rune as a stave with three strokes on each side in a 15-rune inscription; the ending “… gerði mik” is readable, but the opening is uncertain and may combine jǫtunvillur with one binary rune.
- pp. 166–167 document manuscript alternatives (five-, four-, eight-part divisions and ascending numbering); with the same 6:5:5 groups, ascending 123 would make 3/3 = m. These are not secure alternatives in the surveyed epigraphic corpus.
- The cipher-rune system is specifically based on the 16-rune Younger Futhark, whereas LP2's primary alphabet is 29-symbol GP. This cross-alphabet switch still needs a source clue.

This strengthens 3/3 -> þ as the dominant historical epigraphic reading, and gives a close but uncertain repeated-3/3 parallel (B672). It does not turn p0 into a decryption or validate a general 27×27 rule.
